Easiest Microgreens for Beginners (Start With These Four)

Start with radish, broccoli, sunflower, and pea shoots — why they are forgiving, timelines, sowing tips, and what to skip at first.

On this page What “easy” means Radish Broccoli Sunflower Pea shoots What to skip at first

Beginners do not fail microgreens because the idea is hard. They fail because they start with fussy crops, sow like a garden bed, or judge success by Instagram density. If you want a confidence-building first month, grow four forgiving varieties on separate trays: radish, broccoli, sunflower, and pea shoots.

This page explains why each one works in apartments, typical timelines, and practical sowing notes. For the full counter-to-harvest workflow, use how to grow microgreens in a small apartment. For dim rooms, add a budget grow light.

What “easy” means here

An easy beginner crop should: (1) germinate evenly without special chemicals, (2) finish in roughly 7–14 days, (3) tolerate imperfect watering once or twice, (4) taste good enough that you actually eat the harvest, and (5) have seed that is affordable in small bags so mistakes are cheap.

Harder crops are not “bad.” Beet and chard need more blackout management and often leave seed coats on cotyledons. Cilantro is slower. Basil wants warmer, steadier conditions. Save them for tray number five, not tray number one.

1. Radish — fastest feedback loop

Radish microgreens are spicy, colorful, and quick. Many trays are harvestable around day 6–10. The speed matters psychologically: you see whether your watering and light setup works before you lose interest.

Flavor: peppery, like a mild radish root concentrated in the stem and leaf. Excellent on avocado toast, ramen, and grain bowls.

Sowing notes: seed is small-to-medium. Sow a dense single layer. Blackout is usually short. Watch mold if you sow in humid weather with zero airflow — radish canopies get thick fast.

Light: they color up nicely under cool-white LEDs. Stretching means your light is too far or too weak.

If you only grow one crop this week, grow radish. It teaches the whole cycle before your weekend plans change.

2. Broccoli — mild, nutritious, reliable

Broccoli microgreens are a staple for a reason. Germination is generally strong, flavor is mild and green (less polarizing than radish), and the crop is widely documented by extension services for home growers.

Timeline: often ready around day 8–12 when true leaves appear.

Sowing notes: seeds are small; it is easy to oversow into a felt-like mat that traps humidity. Aim for even coverage with a little medium still visible between seeds. No soak required.

Apartment tip: broccoli is a good “office week” crop because it is less dramatic if you bottom-water once a day on a schedule. Pair with a timer-based light so growth stays compact while you are away.

3. Sunflower — crunchy and filling

Sunflower microgreens (often black oil seed sold for microgreens) give you a crunchy, nutty leaf that feels more like a snack than a garnish. They are larger seeded, so the tray looks sparse compared with broccoli even when sown correctly — do not panic and dump extra seed on top.

Prep: soak 6–12 hours, then drain well. Some growers also rinse. Plant in a single tight layer and use blackout with gentle weight so roots drive downward.

Timeline: commonly 8–12 days depending on temperature and how tall you like the shoot.

Watch-outs: seed coats can cling to cotyledons. A gentle mist after uncovering and a bit of airflow helps some coats release; stubborn coats can be slipped off at harvest if needed. Also, sunflower trays are thirsty — underwatering shows up as wilt faster than with broccoli.

4. Pea shoots — sweet vines for stir-fries

Pea shoots are sweet, tendril-y, and familiar to anyone who has ordered them at a restaurant. They need a little more vertical clearance than radish, so they are perfect for a single shelf with a light hung high enough not to press the canopy.

Prep: soak overnight (about 8–12 hours), drain, and sow densely in one layer. Peas appreciate blackout and weight early so roots establish.

Timeline: often 10–14 days. You can cut once, and some growers allow a weaker second cut; as a beginner, take one strong harvest and restart clean.

Flavor uses: eggs, stir-fries, dumplings, and lemony salads. If radish is too spicy for kids or roommates, peas are the diplomats of the tray rack.

What to skip on your first two trays

  • Basil: beautiful later; slower and more environment-sensitive now.
  • Beet / chard: worth learning, but seed clusters and coats frustrate first-timers.
  • Anything unlabeled for food use or coated garden seed not intended for sprouting/microgreens.
  • Mixed “salad blender” packets in one tray until you know each crop’s timing. Mixes look pretty in marketing photos and ripen unevenly in real kitchens.

A simple four-week starter plan

  1. Week 1: sow radish. Practice bottom watering and harvest.
  2. Week 2: sow broccoli the day after you harvest radish. Wash trays in between.
  3. Week 3: sow sunflower (soaked). Note how much more water it wants.
  4. Week 4: sow peas. Adjust light height for taller shoots.

By the end of the month you will know your room’s humidity, your preferred rinse routine, and whether you need a fan. That knowledge is worth more than buying five specialty varieties on day one. Keep notes on sow date, harvest date, and any mold issues — future you will reuse those notes when the site’s daily publishing queue adds more crop-specific pages.

Seed shopping checklist (apartment edition)

Buy smaller bags first. Germination drops as seed ages, and a 1 lb sack of specialty seed is a bad first purchase if you are still learning watering. Look for suppliers that state seed is suitable for microgreens or sprouting, list a packed/tested year, and explain whether a soak is recommended. Store seed cool, dry, and dark in a sealed jar — kitchen steam ruins seed faster than people expect.

Budget roughly enough seed for two full trays of each variety you want to master. That gives you one learning tray and one “do it right” tray without another shipping wait. If a crop molds twice in a row, change airflow and sowing density before you blame the variety.
