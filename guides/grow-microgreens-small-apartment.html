How to Grow Microgreens in a Small Apartment

Step-by-step apartment microgreens: trays, sowing, blackout, watering, light, harvest, and mold prevention on a kitchen counter.

On this page Why apartments work The small-space kit Sow, blackout, light Water without flooding neighbors Harvest and clean-down Common first-crop mistakes

If you live in a studio or a one-bedroom with no balcony, “grow your own food” can sound like content made for people with yards. Microgreens are the exception. They are simply vegetable or herb seedlings harvested young — usually when the first true leaves appear — and they finish in about one to two weeks on a counter, shelf, or sunny sill.

This guide walks through one reliable apartment workflow from empty tray to first harvest. It is written for people who want a repeatable process, not a hobby that takes over the kitchen. When you are ready to choose varieties, see our list of the easiest microgreens for beginners. If your windows are weak in winter, pair this with a cheap grow light setup.

Why apartments work for microgreens

Microgreens do not need deep soil or outdoor weather. Roots stay shallow. The crop is harvested before plants compete for space the way a garden bed would. That means a 10×20 inch tray (or even a cleaned takeout container with drainage holes) is enough surface area to produce a useful handful of greens for salads, eggs, noodles, or sandwiches.

Indoor growing also gives you control. You decide temperature, watering, and light hours. The trade-offs are real too: stagnant apartment air encourages mold, and overwatering is the fastest way to ruin a tray. Treat airflow and moisture as seriously as seed choice.

Apartment rule of thumb: grow on a waterproof surface (tray + drip tray), keep cords and outlets above any possible spill, and run a small clip fan on low for a few hours a day once trays are uncovered.

The small-space kit (buy once, reuse for months)

You do not need a commercial rack on day one. Start with a kit that fits under a cabinet or on a folding table:

  • One shallow tray with drainage holes and one solid drip tray underneath. Standard 1020 trays are common; smaller food-safe containers work if you drill holes.
  • Growing medium: a light seed-starting mix or hydrated coconut coir. Avoid heavy garden soil from outdoors — it stays wet too long and can bring pests indoors.
  • Seeds labeled for microgreens or untreated culinary seed in a dense-sowing crop (radish and broccoli are forgiving starters).
  • Spray bottle for the first days, then bottom-watering into the drip tray.
  • Light: a bright south- or west-facing window, or a simple LED shop light on a timer (12–18 hours).
  • Clean scissors and a colander for harvest day.

Optional but useful in humid buildings: a tiny USB or clip fan aimed past the canopy (not blasting seedlings sideways), and a cheap outlet timer so light cycles stay consistent while you are at work.

Sow, blackout, then give them light

1. Prep the medium

Moisten the mix until it feels like a wrung-out sponge — damp, not dripping. Fill the drained tray with roughly 1.5–2.5 cm (about ½–1 inch) of medium. Level it gently; do not compact it into a brick. Sit that tray inside the solid drip tray.

2. Sow denser than a garden, but not in piles

Scatter seed evenly across the surface. Microgreens are sown much denser than outdoor crops because you harvest them as seedlings. Still, avoid clumps: stacked seeds mold first. For small seeds like broccoli, aim for a single crowded layer with little bare soil showing. For larger seeds like peas or sunflower, a packed single layer with seeds nearly touching is normal — and those seeds often benefit from a 6–12 hour soak before sowing.

3. Press, mist, and blackout

Press seeds lightly into contact with the medium. Mist until the surface is wet. Cover for a dark germination period of about 2–3 days (crop-dependent). Many growers use another tray as a lid, sometimes with light weight for the first day so roots push down instead of lifting the seed coat awkwardly. Check daily. If the surface looks dry, mist again. If you see condensation dripping heavily, crack the cover for airflow for a few minutes.

4. Uncover and light

When most seeds have germinated and you see a pale carpet of stems, remove the cover and move the tray to light. Seedlings will green up over the next day. Keep lights roughly 10–20 cm above the canopy for shop LEDs, and raise the fixture as plants grow so leaves do not scorch or stretch excessively.

Water without flooding the apartment

After blackout, switch to bottom watering. Pour a measured amount into the solid drip tray and let the medium wick moisture upward for 10–20 minutes. Dump any leftover standing water. The goal is even moisture with oxygen still in the root zone. Top-misting after germination can encourage mold on seed coats and dense canopies.

How much water depends on your medium, room humidity, and light heat. Start conservatively — for a 1020 tray, many home growers begin around 300–500 ml and adjust by feel. If the surface is bone dry by evening, add a little more next cycle. If the drip tray still holds a puddle hours later, you overdid it.

Place the whole stack on a baking sheet, silicone mat, or plastic boot tray. Apartments punish quiet leaks: one warped floorboard or stained rental counter is more expensive than a $10 drip catcher.

Harvest and clean-down

Most beginner crops are ready when the first true leaves open and stems look sturdy — often day 7–14 from sow. Cut just above the medium with clean scissors. Do not yank plants; that pulls medium into your salad. Rinse in cool water, spin or pat dry, and refrigerate in a container lined with a paper towel. Flavor is best within a few days.

After harvest, empty spent medium into municipal organics if your city allows it, or bag it with household trash if not. Wash trays with hot soapy water; a diluted vinegar rinse helps if you had any fuzzy mold. Dry completely before the next sow. Clean trays are how small apartments avoid fungus gnats becoming a roommate.

Common first-crop mistakes (and easy fixes)

  • Sowing too thick in a humid bathroom “grow tent.” Dense seed + no airflow = slime. Move to a room with circulating air and thin your sowing rate next time.
  • Leaving trays in standing water overnight. Roots need oxygen. Dump excess.
  • Relying on a dim north window in winter. Seedlings get tall and weak. Add a budget LED; see the grow light guide.
  • Mixing slow and fast crops in one tray. Radish and beet do not finish together. Grow one variety per tray until you know timings.
  • Eating seed untreated for sprouting that was sold only as “garden seed” with coatings. Use seed intended for food harvest / microgreens, and follow the supplier’s guidance.

Your first successful tray teaches more than any gear upgrade. Once you can repeat a clean harvest twice, expand to a second tray on a staggered schedule so you always have something ready to cut. That rhythm — sow, wait, harvest, wash — is the whole hobby at apartment scale.
