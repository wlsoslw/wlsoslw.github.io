Microgreens on a Windowsill Without Grow Lights

You can finish a tray of microgreens with only daylight — if the window is bright enough, you pick the right crops, and you accept slower, leaner growth. This guide covers what works, what fails, and when a cheap shop light stops being optional.

Skip the grow light only if you treat the windowsill as a light source with limits, not as a free pass. Daylight intensity drops fast away from the glass, seasons change the usable hours, and small-seed brassicas stretch toward the brightest edge of the tray. Big-seed crops like peas and sunflower carry more of their own energy, so they forgive weak light better. If you are brand new to trays, skim the apartment starter workflow first, then come back here for the no-equipment path.

Which windows actually work

Orientation and season matter more than “a window that looks bright.”

  • South-facing (Northern Hemisphere) is the strongest choice: several hours of direct sun in clear weather, plus bright indirect light the rest of the day. In summer, midday sun through glass can overheat a shallow tray — pull it a few centimeters back or add a sheer curtain during the hottest hours.
  • East-facing gives cooler morning sun. It is often the best year-round apartment option: enough direct light to keep stems stout without cooking the medium.
  • West-facing works in spring and autumn. Summer afternoon sun can be harsh; winter west light is weaker and often short.
  • North-facing is usually not enough after blackout. Expect pale, leaning, slow trays unless you only grow peas in high summer and still rotate daily.

Glass quality changes the result. Dirty panes, heavy tint, double glazing with Low-E coatings, and deep window recesses all cut usable light. Wipe the glass. Sit the tray as close to the pane as temperature allows — light falls off quickly a foot into the room. In winter you want roughly 4–6 hours of direct sun plus bright ambient light for decent density; in summer, bright indirect light for most of the day can be enough for peas, sunflower, and radish.

Quick test: at midday, hold your hand over the sill. A sharp shadow means usable direct light. A soft, faint shadow means the tray will stretch — pick big-seed crops or add a light.

Crops that tolerate windowsill-only

Match the crop to the light you really have. The ones in our easiest microgreens list still apply, but ranking shifts when daylight is the only source.

  • Pea shoots — best windowsill crop. Large seed, cool-room tolerant, edible even when a bit tall. See the full pea shoots indoor guide.
  • Sunflower — strong second choice if you complete a proper weighted blackout; stems stay thicker than brassicas under the same window. Details in the sunflower soaking and blackout guide.
  • Radish — usually finishes edible on a bright sill. Color may be lighter and stems longer than under a shop light, but flavor stays sharp.
  • Broccoli and other brassicas — grow, but get leggy and pale fast without strong light. Fine as a learning tray on a south/east sill in summer; disappointing in winter.
  • Skip for now: amaranth, basil, and other slow or light-hungry crops. They need consistent intensity that most apartment windows cannot deliver.

Blackout still matters

No grow light does not mean skip the dark phase. Blackout (covering the tray for a few days after sowing) forces roots down, evens germination, and keeps stems from racing toward a window before they have anchored. Use the same cover-and-weight method you would under lights: an upside-down tray or lid, light weight for big seeds, and uncover when shoots push the cover up. Peas need a short blackout; sunflower needs a longer weighted one. After you uncover, move the tray to the sill immediately — delaying light after blackout is what creates the tallest, floppiest stems.

Tray placement and daily habits

  • Distance from glass: keep the tray close for light, but not pressed against cold winter glass. Cold shock plus wet medium invites mold and stalled growth. Leave a finger-width gap, or set the tray on a thin board so the bottom is not sitting on ice-cold tile or stone.
  • Radiators and heat vents: never park a tray above a hot radiator or in the blast of a heater. Medium dries unevenly, edges cook, and mold fills the damp middle.
  • Rotate daily: turn the tray 180° every morning so stems do not permanently lean toward the pane. If one side still stretches, rotate twice a day.
  • Height: a sill that sits the canopy near the brightest part of the glass beats a tray on a table two feet into the room. If the sill is narrow, use a half-size or takeout-style tray — see tray options.
  • Drafts: a gentle room draft is fine; a cold leaky window that chills the tray overnight is not. Move trays indoors at night if the sill drops below about 10 °C (50 °F).

Realistic limits

Windowsill-only trays are a compromise. Expect:

  • Slower finish — often 2–5 days longer than the same crop under a 12–16 hour shop light.
  • Paler color and longer internodes — especially brassicas. Taste is usually still good; appearance is not “market pretty.”
  • Seasonal walls — deep winter north/east apartments often cannot finish dense broccoli. Switch to peas or pause until March–April.
  • Uneven canopy — the window edge grows faster than the room edge unless you rotate.

A cheap LED shop light becomes worth it when you want reliable weekly harvests, denser trays, or brassicas in winter. Our cheap grow light setup is the next step — you do not need a “pro” kit. Until then, stick to peas, sunflower, and radish on your brightest sill and harvest a little earlier if stems start to flop.

Watering and mold on a cool sill

Cool glass and cooler night temperatures slow evaporation. Trays that need daily bottom watering under lights may only need water every other day on a winter sill. Overwatering is the main failure mode here.

  • Prefer bottom watering: soak 10–20 minutes, then dump leftover water.
  • Lift the tray — water only when it feels light.
  • Keep air moving with a small clip fan on low aimed past (not blasting) the canopy. Cool + still + wet is mold weather; see mold prevention.
  • Do not mist after the blackout ends. Surface wetness on a cool sill lingers.

Simple checklist

  1. Pick the brightest sill (south or east first). Wipe the glass.
  2. Choose pea, sunflower, or radish for the first no-light tray.
  3. Sow and complete blackout away from the window if nights are cold.
  4. Uncover and move to the sill the same day; rotate 180° every morning.
  5. Keep a gap from freezing glass; avoid radiators.
  6. Bottom water only when the tray is light; add gentle airflow.
  7. Harvest when height and flavor are good — do not wait for “perfect green” color.
  8. If two trays in a row are pale and floppy, add a shop light or change season/crop.

Bottom line: a bright south or east windowsill can finish pea, sunflower, and radish trays without any grow light if you still blackout, rotate daily, and water for a cool microclimate. Treat brassicas and winter north windows as stretch goals — or as the moment a cheap shop light pays for itself.
